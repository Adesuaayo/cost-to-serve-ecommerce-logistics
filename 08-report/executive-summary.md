# Executive Summary

**Reducing cost-to-serve in e-commerce logistics**
97,300 orders, January 2017 to August 2018. All money in BRL.
Prepared for the Head of Logistics.

## The answer in one line

The money here is not in what freight costs, it is in what lateness
destroys, and lateness is driven by the seller's dispatch leg and by a
delivery promise that is the same length everywhere.

## What the numbers say

Freight consumes 16.64 percent of item value, which invites a
cost-recovery project. That project would fail. Distance, weight and
item count explain 47 percent of freight variation on their own, and the
16 lanes charging above what the model expects account for 28,224 a
year, or 2.12 percent of freight spend. There is no large overcharge to
recover.

What is wrong is the promise. It is padded by 11.81 days on average,
roughly double the 12.5 days orders actually take, and it is still
missed one time in twelve. Cutting the padding uniformly would be a
serious mistake: three days off every lane takes lateness from 8.1 to
13.1 percent, because the padding is covering lane variance rather than
sitting idle. Setting the promise per lane instead cuts lateness to 5.0
percent, about 1,697 fewer late orders a year, and shortens the promise
on 42 of 100 lanes while lengthening it on the rest.

Lateness matters because the damage is a cliff, not a slope. Poor
reviews run 9.2 percent when an order arrives on time and 62.4 percent
when it is late, a relative risk of 6.75. Arriving one day late nearly
triples the poor-review rate. There is no grace period.

The controllable cause is the seller, not the distance. A missed
dispatch deadline makes a late delivery 4.71 times more likely, while
doubling the distance barely moves the odds. The carrier leg consumes 74
percent of the clock and the seller leg only 22, so the seller leg is
the smaller part of the time and the larger part of the failure. It is
also cheap to fix: the top 100 sellers carry 54.3 percent of all
breaches.

One lane carries more of this than any other. SP to RJ is the second
largest lane in the business, 8,138 orders, 15.5 percent late, promising
26.3 days when it needs 40.0.

## What to do, in order

1. **Set the promise per lane**, starting with the 42 lanes whose
   promise shortens, which risks nothing. About 1,697 fewer late orders
   a year at the cost of 2.27 days on the average promise.
2. **Enforce dispatch deadlines on the top 100 sellers.** About 564
   fewer late orders and 300 fewer poor reviews a year.
3. **Renegotiate the 16 north-east lanes.** 28,224 a year. Real, small,
   and harder to execute than either of the above.
4. **Size peak capacity against March**, where lateness runs 2.63 times
   the annual average, not against the average.

## What this analysis cannot tell you

Freight is the amount charged to the customer, not the carrier's cost.
Carrier identity is absent, so renegotiation lands at lane level. No
returns, refunds or service-contact costs exist in the data, so the cost
of a late order is understated and the true value of every
recommendation is higher than modelled. Repeat purchasing is almost
absent, so the revenue effect of review damage rests on a published
benchmark from another industry rather than a measurement from this
business. That figure, 11,122 a year, is the weakest number in the case
and is presented as an order of magnitude with its assumption exposed.
